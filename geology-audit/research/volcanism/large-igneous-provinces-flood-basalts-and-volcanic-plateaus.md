# Large igneous provinces, flood basalts, and volcanic plateaus

## Research status

- **Domain IDs:** M03, linked to F03–F05, G02–G05, T02, V03–V04, X01–X03
- **Status:** IN RESEARCH
- **Stage 2 generation:** BLOCKED
- **Generator implementation:** DEFERRED UNTIL STAGE 1 RESEARCH COMPLETION
- **Purpose:** define large-volume, short-duration magmatic provinces as distributed construction and intrusion systems rather than giant single volcanoes.

## 1. Central rule

A large igneous province is not one eruption and not one edifice.

It is a regional magmatic episode containing some combination of:

- widespread fissure eruptions;
- stacked lava-flow packages;
- dike swarms;
- sill complexes;
- layered intrusions;
- underplating;
- volcanic rifted margins;
- oceanic plateaus;
- silicic centers and ignimbrite provinces;
- crustal heating and metamorphism;
- uplift, subsidence, and basin interaction;
- atmospheric and oceanic forcing;
- later erosion, burial, rifting, and fragmentation.

The dominant surface expression may be a plateau or plain, while much of the magma remains intrusive.

## 2. Scale and tempo

LIPs are defined by unusually large areal and volumetric magmatic output concentrated into geologically short pulses relative to ordinary background magmatism.

Important variables:

- total province area;
- total intrusive and extrusive volume;
- fraction emplaced during peak pulses;
- duration of individual eruptive pulses;
- total province lifespan;
- vent and fissure distribution;
- crustal setting;
- preservation and later fragmentation.

Stage 1 must not encode one universal size or duration threshold across all rocky worlds.

## 3. Continental flood-basalt provinces

Potential architecture:

- repeated sheet-like basalt flows;
- fissures and dike swarms;
- deeply incised flow stacks producing stepped topography after erosion;
- lava filling pre-existing valleys and basins;
- buried landscapes and drainage networks;
- extensive sills in sedimentary basins;
- central and peripheral intrusive complexes;
- localized silicic volcanism;
- regional uplift or subsidence before, during, or after eruption;
- later rifting and continental breakup.

A mature eroded flood-basalt province may appear as:

- plateaus and mesas;
- escarpments;
- inverted valleys;
- canyon-exposed flow layers;
- dike-controlled ridges;
- sedimentary basins overlying or flanking the province.

It should not look like one giant shield.

## 4. Oceanic plateaus

Oceanic plateaus may involve unusually thick oceanic crust produced by massive magmatism.

Potential consequences:

- broad elevated seafloor region;
- thickened crust;
- reduced water depth relative to surrounding abyssal floor;
- internal volcanic construction and seamounts;
- later subsidence and sediment cover;
- collision with subduction zones;
- resistance to subduction or modification of slab geometry;
- accretion to continental margins;
- fragmentation by later spreading.

The plateau is not merely a smooth bathymetric high. Its crustal thickness, age, load, sediment, and tectonic history must remain explicit.

## 5. Volcanic rifted margins

Large magmatism during breakup may create:

- seaward-dipping lava sequences;
- thick igneous crust;
- dike swarms;
- underplating;
- broad volcanic plateaus;
- margin uplift and later subsidence;
- asymmetry between conjugate margins;
- early flood-basalt fields connected to rifting.

These systems must remain linked to the rift/breakup history rather than added as independent volcanic decoration.

## 6. Intrusive architecture

A large fraction of LIP magma may be emplaced below the surface.

Important components:

- giant radiating or linear dike swarms;
- sill complexes;
- layered mafic intrusions;
- crustal underplating;
- magma reservoirs and feeder zones;
- contact aureoles;
- thermal weakening and metamorphism.

Potential downstream effects:

- crustal thickening;
- regional uplift or subsidence;
- volatile release from heated sediment;
- later mineralization;
- structural inheritance guiding rifts and erosion;
- high-density bodies affecting gravity and support.

Generator obligation:

- visible lava volume must not stand in for the total magmatic event.

## 7. Eruption style and flow architecture

Flood-basalt provinces are commonly built by many fissure-fed flows rather than one continuous flood.

Potential flow behavior:

- long low-viscosity sheets;
- compound flow fields;
- lava inflation;
- channelized flow;
- ponding in basins;
- topographic confinement;
- flow-front stacking;
- hiatuses with soil, sediment, or erosion between pulses.

A province can be regionally broad while individual flows remain controlled by local topography.

## 8. Silicic and mixed large provinces

Some large provinces contain substantial silicic magmatism or evolve through basaltic heating and crustal melting.

Possible expressions:

- ignimbrite sheets;
- caldera complexes;
- rhyolitic plateaus;
- domes and ash-flow deposits;
- mixed intrusive and extrusive crustal construction.

Therefore “LIP” cannot be reduced to dark basalt color or flat flood-lava terrain.

## 9. Climate and environmental interaction

Large igneous provinces can influence climate and oceans through:

- direct volcanic gas release;
- sulfur aerosols;
- carbon dioxide;
- halogens;
- contact metamorphism of volatile-rich sediment;
- ocean fertilization or deoxygenation pathways;
- long-term silicate weathering of fresh basalt;
- repeated pulses rather than one instantaneous forcing.

These interactions depend on:

- eruption and intrusion tempo;
- host-rock composition;
- latitude and climate state;
- atmospheric mass and chemistry;
- ocean circulation;
- weathering efficiency.

Generator obligation:

- global climate forcing must be a declared optional consequence of sufficiently large events, not a universal property of every volcanic province.

## 10. LIP and plume relationship

Some models link LIPs to plume-head arrival followed by a longer hotspot track. Other provinces may involve rifting, plate-boundary reorganization, lithospheric processes, or mixed causes.

Stage 1 must preserve:

- plume-head branch;
- rift-assisted branch;
- lithosphere-controlled branch;
- uncertain/mixed branch;
- optional later hotspot-track connection.

No mandatory LIP-to-hotspot-tail relation is approved.

## 11. Resurfacing and age patterns

Large provinces can reset surface age across broad regions through:

- lava burial;
- ash/ignimbrite deposition;
- intrusion-driven deformation;
- erosion and sediment redistribution;
- later faulting and rifting.

A province should produce a patchwork of:

- primary surfaces;
- buried older terrain;
- younger vents and flows;
- erosional windows;
- sediment-covered sectors;
- tectonically reworked margins.

A uniform age disk is invalid.

## 12. Fragmentation and inheritance

Later tectonics may split one province across:

- conjugate continental margins;
- separate continents;
- accreted terranes;
- ocean basins;
- subduction zones.

Matching dike swarms, ages, and geochemistry can record former continuity even when modern topography differs.

WorldWright consequence:

- one ancient province can have multiple descendant fragments with shared ancestry but independent later landscapes.

## 13. Erosion and sediment response

Flood-basalt and volcanic-plateau terrains may evolve through:

- canyon incision;
- stepped escarpments;
- mass wasting;
- weathering of flow tops;
- resistant caprock and inverted relief;
- large sediment pulses;
- river diversion by lava dams;
- groundwater and spring systems along flow contacts;
- coastal cliffs and submarine sediment delivery.

The province cannot be treated as permanently flat and featureless.

## 14. Valid broad and radial forms

LIPs create important valid exceptions to anti-blob rules.

Potential valid forms:

- broad volcanic plateau;
- plume-head-scale uplift or thermal swell;
- radiating dike swarm;
- distributed fissure field;
- large oceanic plateau;
- ring-like or radial feeder organization around a source region.

Validity requirements:

- province-scale magmatic event record;
- intrusive/extrusive budget;
- temporal pulses;
- crustal modification;
- later erosion/burial/fragmentation;
- shape not derived solely from one isotropic height kernel.

## 15. Multiscale visual obligations

### Planetary scale

- province distribution compatible with thermal and tectonic regime;
- broad resurfacing and crustal-thickness effects;
- fragmented descendants across later plate reconstructions;
- possible climate coupling for extreme events.

### Continental/ocean-basin scale

- flood-basalt plateaus;
- dike and sill provinces;
- oceanic plateaus;
- volcanic margins;
- linked rift and hotspot systems;
- sediment and drainage reorganization.

### Regional scale

- fissure systems;
- stacked flow packages;
- caldera/ignimbrite centers;
- escarpments and mesas;
- intrusive uplifts;
- lava-filled basins.

### Local scale

- flow lobes and channels;
- dikes and sills;
- lava tubes;
- columnar-joint scarps;
- paleosols and sediment interbeds;
- springs and waterfalls at flow contacts.

## 16. Threshold and interaction axes

Future Stage 2 cases must cross:

- total magma supply;
- intrusive/extrusive fraction;
- pulse tempo;
- continental versus oceanic setting;
- rift association;
- plume/source model;
- sedimentary-host volatility;
- crust/lithosphere strength;
- climate and erosion;
- age and fragmentation;
- active, waning, fossil, buried, or reactivated state.

No universal province-size threshold is approved for non-Earth worlds.

## 17. Generator obligation specification

### Canonical state required

- province identity and ancestry;
- source-model branch and confidence;
- event/pulse timeline;
- fissure and feeder network;
- intrusive/extrusive volume budget;
- lava/ignimbrite deposit fields;
- crustal underplating/thickening;
- host-basin and volatile interaction;
- uplift/subsidence/support history;
- fragmentation and descendant records;
- erosion, burial, and preservation state.

### Required process fields

- regional melt supply;
- fissure localization;
- flow emplacement and ponding;
- sill/dike intrusion;
- underplating;
- resurfacing and age reset;
- thermal/metamorphic influence;
- load/support response;
- erosion and sediment production;
- optional atmospheric/ocean forcing.

### Downstream consequences

- crustal thickness and density;
- bedrock and deposit elevation;
- basin fill and drainage diversion;
- flexure and subsidence;
- surface age/material;
- climate/ocean perturbation where enabled;
- later rift, collision, or subduction interaction.

### Conservation/reconciliation

```text
province magma supply
≈ intrusive volume
+ extrusive deposit volume
+ unresolved/exported fraction
```

Erosion and tectonic removal must preserve descendant or exported mass/provenance records.

## 18. Procedural failure signatures

### 18.1 Giant single volcano
A province is represented as one central edifice.

### 18.2 Uniform lava blanket
All flows have one age, thickness, direction, and texture.

### 18.3 Radial disk uplift
The province is only a circular plateau kernel without feeders, pulses, or crustal modification.

### 18.4 Surface-only LIP
No dikes, sills, underplating, host-rock interaction, or gravity/crust effect.

### 18.5 Mandatory hotspot tail
Every LIP creates a later linear volcanic chain.

### 18.6 No paleotopography
Lava ignores valleys, basins, slopes, and barriers.

### 18.7 Uniform age reset
The whole province has one sharp boundary and one surface age.

### 18.8 No climate or sediment context
Large events never affect atmosphere/ocean, or every event affects them identically.

### 18.9 Fragmentation duplicates terrain
Descendant fragments remain visually identical after later tectonic histories.

- one-age mask;

4. How should intrusive heating and volatile release be approximated?

## 21. Conclusions safe enough to carry forward

### High confidence

- LIPs are distributed magmatic provinces, not single volcanoes or eruptions;
- intrusive architecture is a major part of the system;
- volcanic plateaus and oceanic plateaus require crustal and event history;
- flows interact with pre-existing topography and are emplaced in pulses;
- later erosion, burial, rifting, and fragmentation strongly alter appearance;
- extreme provinces may affect atmosphere and oceans, but the magnitude depends on tempo and host context.

### Model-dependent or incomplete

- plume-head causation;
- exact size/duration thresholds;
- environmental forcing magnitude;
- quantitative source-to-flow mapping;
- cross-planet preservation.

### Not approved

- giant radial lava masks;
- one-cone LIPs;
- mandatory plume tails;
- generator implementation;
- image generation.
