# Isostasy, lithospheric flexure, and load response

## Research status

- **Domain IDs:** V01, V02, linked to F05, C01–C05, T05, O01–O05, I01–I02
- **Status:** IN RESEARCH
- **Stage 2 generation:** BLOCKED
- **Purpose:** distinguish local buoyant compensation from regional elastic/viscoelastic bending and from active, transient, or mantle-supported topography.

## 1. Central rule

Topography is not only a surface shape. It is a load and support problem.

A highland, basin, volcanic edifice, ice sheet, sediment pile, or impact structure can deform the lithosphere differently depending on:

- load magnitude and geometry;
- density contrast;
- elastic strength;
- rheological layering;
- thermal state;
- loading rate and duration;
- whether the load is on the surface or buried;
- whether active tectonic or mantle forces are also present.

WorldWright must therefore classify the **support mechanism** and **load history** separately from final elevation.

## 2. Isostasy as a limiting equilibrium concept

Isostasy describes gravitational compensation: lateral columns tend toward comparable pressure at a compensation depth when sufficient time and appropriate rheology allow adjustment.

It is not a universal statement that every location is in perfect equilibrium.

### 2.1 Airy-style thickness compensation

Idealized assumptions:

- crustal density is approximately constant laterally;
- high terrain is supported by thicker crustal roots;
- low terrain corresponds to thinner crust.

Useful for:

- first-order continental roots;
- some crustal plateaus;
- broad compensated highlands.

Unsafe uses:

- active collision belts treated as static columns;
- volcanic edifices with elastic support;
- impact basins with mantle uplift;
- dynamic swells or active subsidence;
- direct conversion of crust thickness into elevation.

### 2.2 Pratt-style density compensation

Idealized assumptions:

- compensation arises mainly from lateral density differences above a common depth;
- lower-density columns stand higher.

Useful for:

- thermal or compositional buoyancy end members;
- crustal provinces where density differs materially;
- conceptual separation of thickness and density effects.

Unsafe uses:

- treating temperature or composition as a direct height texture;
- assuming a single compensation depth for active heterogeneous lithosphere;
- ignoring elastic or dynamic support.

### 2.3 Mixed compensation

Real regions may combine:

- crustal-thickness variation;
- crustal-density variation;
- lithospheric mantle density variation;
- elastic support;
- buried loads;
- active mantle flow.

Stage 2 must allow mixed-support families rather than forcing every case into Airy or Pratt labels.

## 3. Flexural isostasy and regional load response

A lithosphere with finite strength can distribute a load over a broad region by bending.

This creates spatially linked features rather than one isolated high or low.

## 3.1 Basic geometric response

Under a concentrated or belt-like positive load, a flexing plate may produce:

- downwarping beneath or adjacent to the load;
- a basin or moat;
- an outer forebulge or peripheral uplift;
- a farther back-bulge depression;
- wavelength controlled by flexural rigidity and density contrast.

The exact geometry depends on dimensionality, load shape, plate boundaries, rheology, sediment fill, and active forces.

### WorldWright implication

A loaded mountain belt, volcano, island chain, ice cap, or impact structure should not create only a positive elevation feature. Its mechanically coupled surrounding response may be equally diagnostic.

## 3.2 Effective elastic thickness

Effective elastic thickness (`Te`) is the thickness of an idealized elastic plate that reproduces observed flexural behavior.

It is not identical to:

- crustal thickness;
- total lithosphere thickness;
- brittle-layer thickness;
- depth to a specific isotherm;
- seismogenic thickness.

`Te` can reflect integrated rheology, thermal state, coupling, damage, loading history, and inversion assumptions.

### High `Te` tendency

- broader flexural wavelength;
- load distributed over a wider region;
- shallower, wider deflection for a comparable idealized load;
- stronger preservation of regional plate-like bending.

### Low `Te` tendency

- more localized bending;
- shorter wavelength;
- behavior closer to local compensation in the weak-plate limit;
- potentially stronger local subsidence near a load.

These are tendencies, not universal visual thresholds.

## 3.3 Inference uncertainty

Gravity/topography methods infer `Te` through admittance, coherence, or statistical load models. Simons and Olhede emphasize that flexural-rigidity inversion can be non-unique and entangled with:

- initial loading fraction;
- surface versus subsurface loading;
- load correlation;
- spatial windowing;
- anisotropy;
- spectral assumptions.

Stage 1 rule:

> Every elastic-thickness value used in Stage 2 must carry its inference method, wavelength range, load model, and uncertainty.

## 4. Foreland systems

A growing orogenic load can bend adjacent lithosphere and create a foreland basin system.

A full system may include:

- wedge-top region over the deformed belt;
- foredeep adjacent to the load;
- forebulge farther from the load;
- back-bulge basin beyond the forebulge;
- sediment derived from the mountain belt and redistributed through the basin.

## 4.1 Visual obligations

At continental/regional scale, a mature foreland response tends to be:

- elongated along the mountain belt;
- laterally variable with load and plate rigidity;
- asymmetric across strike;
- partly filled or masked by sediment;
- integrated with drainage and thrust-front migration.

The foredeep is not necessarily an exposed lake or obvious topographic trench. Sediment can fill accommodation space and produce a low-relief plain over a deep structural basin.

## 4.2 Why this matters for collision-belt auditing

A collision belt and its foreland cannot be judged as separate decorative shapes.

Future rules should ask:

- is a basin/lowland mechanically plausible relative to the load?
- does its orientation track the belt?
- does sediment supply respond to the orogen?
- does the basin migrate or fill through time?
- is a forebulge expected, preserved, buried, or eroded?

## 4.3 Procedural failure risk

A generator may create a circular mountain blob plus a symmetric moat and call it flexure.

That is not sufficient. A collision-related foreland system should inherit the geometry and segmentation of the load, usually producing belt-parallel rather than globally concentric response.

## 5. Volcanic and island loading

Large volcanic edifices and island chains can flex oceanic or planetary lithosphere.

Potential responses include:

- subsidence beneath the edifice;
- peripheral moat;
- outer arch or flexural bulge;
- fracture patterns influenced by bending stress;
- sediment accumulation in flexural lows;
- drowned lower flanks through subsidence.

### Radial exception

Unlike collision belts, isolated volcanoes may legitimately produce approximately radial loads and moats.

Audit implication:

- radial flexure is valid for an isolated radial load;
- radial flexure is suspicious when the declared load is an elongated orogenic belt;
- the load geometry and response geometry must correspond.

## 6. Ice and surface-mass loading

Ice sheets, lakes, sediment piles, and water loads can flex the lithosphere and produce delayed rebound after unloading.

Relevant effects include:

- depressed crust beneath large ice masses;
- peripheral bulges;
- postglacial uplift;
- shoreline tilting;
- drainage reorganization;
- changing relative sea level.

Stage 1 must later cross flexure with glacial and sea-level domains. These features should not be assigned solely to tectonics.

## 7. Sediment loading and basin self-modification

A basin does not merely receive sediment passively. Sediment mass can add load and increase subsidence.

Feedback chain:

```text
tectonic or flexural accommodation
→ sediment deposition
→ additional load
→ further subsidence
→ changing drainage and shoreline position
```

This means a final basin shape may differ substantially from the initial mechanical deflection.

WorldWright implication:

- structural basin depth;
- sediment-fill thickness;
- water depth;
- final surface elevation;

must be separate fields.

## 8. Impact loading and mantle plugs

Large impacts excavate crust, uplift mantle, emplace melt, fracture the lithosphere, and create mass anomalies.

Deng et al. show that ordinary surface-load flexural models may fail for large Martian basins unless impact-related mantle uplift is included.

Stage 1 rule:

> Impact basins require impact-specific loading models and cannot be treated as ordinary negative surface loads.

Potential coupled features:

- excavated depression;
- central or mantle uplift;
- crustal thinning;
- melt sheet;
- ring faults;
- positive or negative gravity anomaly;
- later volcanic or sedimentary infill;
- flexural response outside the basin.

## 9. Time-dependent response

The lithosphere is not perfectly elastic over all time scales.

Possible response sequence:

1. rapid elastic deformation;
2. viscoelastic relaxation;
3. ductile flow at depth;
4. erosion or redistribution of the load;
5. sedimentary or volcanic infill;
6. rebound after unloading;
7. inherited fossil flexure after the original load changes.

Stage 2 sequences must therefore distinguish:

- active loading;
- peak load;
- relaxation;
- unloading;
- fossil flexural architecture.

## 10. Multiscale visual consequences

## 10.1 Planetary scale

- long-wavelength bending beneath giant volcanic or ice loads;
- broad basin and swell systems;
- load-related regional sea-level differences;
- global contrasts in elastic thickness.

## 10.2 Continental/basin scale

- foreland basins;
- rift-flank and margin warping;
- volcanic moat-and-arch systems;
- glacial depression and peripheral uplift;
- impact-basin compensation.

## 10.3 Regional scale

- foredeep/forebulge geometry;
- flexural scarps or tilted strata;
- drainage deflection;
- localized sediment depocenters;
- drowned or uplifted shorelines.

## 10.4 Local scale

Flexure alone often does not specify fine texture. Local appearance depends on:

- faulting;
- erosion;
- sedimentation;
- lithology;
- coastline position;
- volcanic or glacial processes.

A broad flexural field should not be directly rendered as smooth concentric contour bands without these mediating processes.

## 11. Threshold axes to research

## 11.1 Load magnitude

- negligible: no resolvable regional response;
- low: subtle tilting or subsidence;
- moderate: coherent basin/bulge response;
- high: strong deflection, faulting, sediment trapping;
- extreme: nonlinear yielding, fracture, delamination, or other breakdown of simple elastic assumptions.

## 11.2 Load width and shape

- point/radial load: radial moat/bulge tendencies;
- line/belt load: elongated basin and bulge tendencies;
- distributed load: broad warping;
- irregular segmented load: segmented and overlapping response.

## 11.3 Elastic strength

- weak: localized compensation;
- intermediate: visible regional flexure;
- strong: broad wavelength and distributed support;
- heterogeneous: variable basin width, segmented bulges, asymmetric response.

## 11.4 Loading rate and age

- rapid young loading: strong disequilibrium and elastic signature;
- long-lived loading: relaxation and sediment feedback;
- removed load: rebound and fossil warping;
- repeated loading: superposed flexural histories.

No universal numeric bins are approved yet.

## 12. Required audit metadata

Future reference cases involving loads should include:

- load type;
- load geometry;
- load age;
- loading/unloading state;
- surface versus subsurface load fraction;
- effective elastic thickness and uncertainty if known;
- assumed rheology;
- compensation style;
- flexural wavelength;
- basin-fill state;
- active tectonic or dynamic support;
- expected visible scale.

## 13. Procedural failure signatures

### 13.1 Universal symmetric moat
Every highland receives the same circular depression regardless of load shape.

### 13.2 Flexure painted from distance only
A radial or belt distance transform creates idealized rings with no mechanical or geological mediation.

### 13.3 Missing paired response
A large load produces no surrounding subsidence, tilting, or stress response where one should be expected.

### 13.4 Wrong wavelength
A strong lithosphere produces extremely tight local bending, or a weak lithosphere produces continent-scale smooth warping without justification.

### 13.5 Sediment ignored
Deep structural basins remain permanently exposed as water-filled trenches despite high sediment supply.

### 13.6 Load and response mismatch
An elongated or segmented load produces a circular response, or an isolated radial volcano produces an implausibly straight foreland basin.

### 13.7 Elastic thickness used as a texture control
`Te` changes roughness or color directly rather than load-response geometry.

## 14. Stage 2 coverage obligations

Future reference generation must include controlled families for:

- local Airy-like thickness compensation;
- density-dominated compensation;
- mixed thickness/density compensation;
- weak, intermediate, and strong flexural plates;
- line-load orogenic forelands;
- radial volcanic loading;
- ice loading and rebound;
- sediment-loaded basins;
- impact-specific compensation;
- active, relaxed, unloaded, and fossil states;
- heterogeneous elastic thickness.

### Required negative controls

- circular moat around a collision belt;
- direct crust-root height painting;
- basin depth equated with water depth;
- universal fixed-width foreland basin;
- impact basin treated as ordinary flexure;
- idealized rings visible in final terrain;
- no sediment feedback.

8. How should active faulting emerge from bending stress?
9. How should water and ice loads be coupled to evolving shorelines?

## 16. Conclusions safe enough to carry forward

### High confidence

- local isostasy and regional flexure are distinct support modes;
- effective elastic thickness is not crustal thickness;
- load geometry should control response geometry;
- foreland basins are mechanically coupled to orogenic loads;
- sediment fill can hide structural basin depth;
- volcanic and impact loads require different exception families;
- simple flexural inversion is assumption-sensitive and potentially non-unique.

### Model-dependent or incomplete

- exact `Te` values and their spatial variation;
- viscoelastic relaxation time;
- contribution of buried/subsurface loads;
- visibility of forebulges;
- cross-planet scaling of flexural wavelength.

### Not approved

- universal flexural width constants;
- automatic moat generation;
- direct `Te` rendering;
- generic impact-as-load behavior;
- numeric Stage 2 gates;
- reference-image generation.
