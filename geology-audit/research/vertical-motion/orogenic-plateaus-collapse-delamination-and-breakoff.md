# Orogenic plateaus, collapse, delamination, and breakoff

## Research status

- **Domain IDs:** V04, linked to T05, V01–V03, M01–M03, X03
- **Status:** IN RESEARCH
- **Stage 2 generation:** BLOCKED
- **Purpose:** define how thickened collision zones broaden, extend, lose dense roots, change support mechanisms, and evolve after peak convergence.

## 1. Central rule

High topography does not imply one stable support mechanism through time.

A collision plateau or thickened orogen may evolve through changing combinations of:

- crustal thickening and isostatic support;
- lithospheric underthrusting;
- elastic or flexural support;
- lower-crustal flow;
- mantle-flow support;
- magmatic addition;
- removal of dense lower crust or mantle lithosphere;
- slab breakoff;
- gravitational spreading and extension;
- erosion and sediment redistribution.

The final terrain can retain similar broad elevation while the underlying cause changes. Stage 2 must therefore record support history, not only current height.

## 2. Plateau formation is not a single process

Broad high plateaus may arise from:

- distributed crustal shortening over a wide region;
- multiple stacked crustal wedges;
- underthrust continental lithosphere;
- magmatic thickening;
- lower-crustal redistribution;
- inherited pre-collision highlands;
- delamination or foundering followed by buoyant rebound;
- dynamic mantle support;
- combinations of these mechanisms.

### Audit requirement

A plateau case must specify:

- whether high elevation is primarily crustal, lithospheric, dynamic, magmatic, or mixed;
- whether support is active or inherited;
- whether the plateau interior is deforming, extending, stable, or collapsing;
- how the plateau connects to frontal mountain belts and basins.

## 3. Plateau interiors

Plateau interiors need not resemble continuous sharp mountains.

Possible expressions include:

- broad low-relief highlands;
- internally drained basins;
- fault-bounded ranges;
- volcanic fields;
- extensional grabens;
- strike-slip systems;
- salt lakes and sediment-filled depressions;
- inherited terranes and sutures with weak surface expression;
- localized high ranges at margins or internal structures.

A plateau is therefore a regional elevation and deformation state, not a uniformly rugged texture.

## 4. Gravitational potential energy and extension

Thickened, elevated crust stores gravitational potential energy.

If internal strength or boundary forces permit, the orogen may spread or extend even while plate convergence continues.

Potential surface expressions:

- normal faults in the high interior;
- rift-like basins parallel or oblique to the orogen;
- metamorphic-core-complex exhumation;
- widening of the plateau;
- lowering of peak relief while the mean elevation remains high;
- outward flow or extrusion of weak middle/lower crust;
- interaction with active thrusting at the margins.

Stage 1 rule:

> Extension inside a convergent orogen is not automatically contradictory. The audit must compare the extension with plateau thickness, rheology, gravitational state, and boundary kinematics.

## 5. Syn-convergent versus post-orogenic collapse

### Syn-convergent extension

May occur while convergence continues because:

- the plateau is overthickened;
- the interior is hot and weak;
- strain is partitioned;
- lower-crustal flow redistributes material;
- boundary geometry changes;
- gravitational forces exceed local resistance.

### Post-orogenic collapse

May occur after external convergence weakens or ends.

Potential consequences:

- widespread normal faulting;
- basin formation;
- rapid exhumation;
- magmatism;
- thinning and lateral spreading of crust;
- transition toward later rifting.

The two states may look similar locally but have different plate-scale contexts.

## 6. Lower-crustal flow

Hot, thick crust may weaken enough for material to move laterally at depth.

Potential effects:

- smoothing of crustal-thickness gradients;
- support or redistribution of plateau elevation;
- doming or uplift away from the main shortening front;
- exhumation through paired normal and thrust structures;
- decoupling between upper-crustal fault patterns and deeper mass transfer.

This mechanism remains model-dependent in individual orogens.

WorldWright implication:

- lower-crustal flow may modify broad support fields;
- it must not appear as a visible smooth radial height kernel;
- its consequences should emerge through crustal thickness, uplift, extension, and exhumation patterns.

## 7. Delamination and foundering

Dense lower crust or mantle lithosphere may become gravitationally unstable and detach or sink.

Potential causes include:

- crustal/lithospheric thickening;
- phase changes increasing density;
- thermal or chemical weakening;
- melt infiltration;
- inherited weak zones;
- slab or root geometry.

Potential surface consequences include:

- uplift from removal of dense material;
- subsidence during loading or removal stages;
- volcanism and crustal melting as hot asthenosphere rises;
- basin reorganization;
- plateau-margin retreat or rejuvenation;
- localized rather than uniform response.

The southern Puna tomography study identified a high-velocity body interpreted as delaminated material and linked it with melt, volcanism, and high plateau topography. This is an observationally constrained interpretation, not proof that all plateaus delaminate. 

## 8. Slab breakoff

After continental collision, the dense oceanic slab may detach from buoyant continental lithosphere.

Possible consequences:

- rapid change in slab pull;
- asthenospheric upwelling through the slab gap;
- magmatic pulses;
- uplift or subsidence depending on timing and geometry;
- changes in convergence and stress transmission;
- seismic and metamorphic consequences;
- cessation or migration of arc magmatism.

Slab breakoff is a transient event, not a permanent broad uplift field.

## 9. Eclogitization and density instability

Thickened mafic lower crust may transform into denser mineral assemblages under high pressure.

Potential effects:

- increased negative buoyancy;
- foundering or delamination;
- reduction of crustal root support;
- possible surface subsidence followed by rebound;
- magmatic consequences after asthenospheric replacement.

The process depends strongly on:

- composition;
- pressure–temperature path;
- hydration;
- reaction kinetics;
- crustal residence time.

No direct crust-thickness threshold is approved.

## 10. Plateau-margin retreat and rejuvenation

Removal or weakening of dense lithosphere may propagate along plateau margins.

Potential surface expressions:

- renewed uplift of an ancient plateau margin;
- inward migration of volcanism or extension;
- river incision and canyon development;
- retreating escarpments;
- changing drainage divides;
- asymmetric rejuvenation.

This creates an important exception for old orogens: modern relief may be younger than the original collision.

## 11. Mantle support versus crustal support

A plateau may be supported by:

- thick buoyant crust;
- strong lithosphere;
- dynamic mantle stress;
- thermal buoyancy;
- removal of dense lithosphere;
- mixed mechanisms.

Similar elevation does not guarantee similar support.

### Future audit requirement

Reference cases should include causal layers such as:

- crust thickness;
- density/buoyancy class;
- lithosphere thickness;
- mantle anomaly/support class;
- current uplift/subsidence state;
- volcanism;
- extension and faulting;
- uncertainty or competing interpretation.

## 12. Collapse morphology

A collapsing or extending plateau may contain:

- long normal-fault systems;
- grabens and half-grabens;
- metamorphic domes;
- volcanic fields;
- internally drained basins;
- uplifted ranges separating subsiding basins;
- inherited compressional structures cut by younger extension.

The pattern is commonly directional or structurally inherited, not a uniform flattening of the entire orogen.

## 13. Drainage and sediment response

Uplift, collapse, and root removal may:

- reorganize drainage divides;
- create new internally drained basins;
- accelerate incision during rejuvenation;
- reverse or capture rivers;
- produce large sediment pulses;
- expose former basin deposits;
- alter relative sea level downstream.

A plateau-lowering or uplift event without hydrologic and sedimentary consequences is incomplete.

## 14. Temporal states

Stage 2 must eventually distinguish:

1. active thickening;
2. peak plateau development;
3. early internal extension;
4. active delamination/foundering or breakoff;
5. magmatic/uplift response;
6. mature collapse;
7. waning relief and basin fill;
8. fossil plateau or rejuvenated ancient highland.

These states should not be generated by simply scaling one heightfield.

## 15. Multiscale visual obligations

### Planetary scale

- broad high region connected to collision or inherited crustal architecture;
- asymmetry in support and collapse;
- coherent relations to plate boundaries, mantle anomalies, and drainage outlets.

### Continental scale

- plateau margins, frontal belts, interior basins, and major strike-slip/normal-fault systems;
- spatially variable uplift and extension;
- volcanism tied to mantle/lithosphere evolution.

### Regional scale

- fault-bounded basins;
- metamorphic domes;
- volcanic provinces;
- uplifted or subsiding blocks;
- river gorges and rejuvenated escarpments.

### Local scale

- normal-fault scarps;
- basin fans and lake deposits;
- volcanic centers;
- landslides and incision;
- cross-cut compressional and extensional structures.

## 18. Procedural failure signatures

### 18.1 Plateau by blur
A mountain belt is widened and smoothed without crustal, lithospheric, basin, or fault logic.

### 18.2 Uniform plateau interior
The entire highland has identical roughness, elevation, and deformation.

### 18.3 Collapse as global smoothing
Orogenic collapse merely reduces elevation everywhere rather than producing directional extension and basins.

### 18.4 Delamination uplift disk
A circular mask raises terrain directly over a supposed root-removal center.

### 18.5 Breakoff as permanent dome
A transient slab event becomes a long-lived symmetric uplift kernel.

### 18.6 Extension disconnected from convergence
Normal faults are sprinkled randomly without relation to plateau thickness or stress state.

### 18.7 Volcanism disconnected from removal
Magmatic provinces ignore delamination/breakoff geometry and timing.

### 18.8 Support double-counting
Crust thickness, thermal buoyancy, mantle support, and root removal all independently add full uplift over the same region.

### 18.9 No drainage response
Large plateau uplift or collapse leaves drainage and sediment unchanged.

## 20. Unresolved questions

1. Which support mechanisms can WorldWright represent explicitly without overcomplicating the generator?
2. How should uncertainty between crustal and mantle support be stored?
3. Which plateau-interior fault patterns are robust enough for Stage 2 families?
4. How should lower-crustal flow be parameterized and validated?
5. What observable sequence distinguishes delamination, foundering, and slab breakoff?
6. How should transient uplift evolve through erosion and sedimentation?
7. Which magmatic responses are required versus optional?
8. How should eclogitization be represented without a simplistic thickness trigger?
9. How should plateau-margin rejuvenation interact with ancient orogen research?
10. Which non-Earth tectonic regimes can produce plateau-like highlands without collision?

## 21. Conclusions safe enough to carry forward

### High confidence

- plateau support can change through time;
- extension may coexist with convergence in thickened orogens;
- collapse is structural evolution, not simple smoothing;
- delamination/foundering can couple uplift, subsidence, magmatism, and drainage change;
- slab breakoff is transient and spatially structured;
- similar broad highlands may have different support mechanisms;
- hydrology and sediment must respond to vertical-motion changes.

### Model-dependent or incomplete

- support beneath specific plateaus;
- timing and geometry of lower-crustal flow;
- frequency and scale of delamination;
- surface response to slab breakoff;
- eclogitization kinetics and thresholds.

### Not approved

- universal plateau-support model;
- direct root-removal uplift masks;
- numeric scoring thresholds;
- image generation.
