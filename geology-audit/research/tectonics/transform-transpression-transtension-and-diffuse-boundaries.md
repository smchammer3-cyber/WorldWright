# Transform, transpression, transtension, and diffuse plate-boundary systems

## Research status

- **Domain IDs:** T06–T08, linked to C05, T01–T05, V01–V03, X03
- **Status:** IN RESEARCH
- **Stage 2 generation:** BLOCKED
- **Purpose:** define how strike-slip motion, obliquity, stepovers, distributed strain, microplates, and triple junctions create complex but causally organized terrain.

## 1. Central rule

A transform boundary is not simply a straight fault line with zero topographic effect.

Strike-slip systems may produce:

- narrow fault traces;
- broad fault zones;
- offset landforms and drainage;
- pull-apart basins;
- pressure ridges;
- transpressional mountain belts;
- transtensional rifts;
- rotating blocks;
- flower structures;
- releasing and restraining bends;
- microplates and slivers;
- distributed shear across wide plate-boundary zones.

The surface expression depends on fault geometry, slip sense, obliquity, inherited structure, crust type, sediment, erosion, and time.

## 2. Oceanic transforms versus fracture zones

At mid-ocean ridges:

- active transform faults connect offset spreading segments;
- inactive fracture zones extend away from the active transform into older crust;
- the active segment accommodates opposite relative motion across the fault;
- fracture zones preserve age, depth, and structural contrasts but are no longer plate boundaries.

WorldWright must distinguish:

- active transform segment;
- inactive fracture-zone trace;
- ridge-transform intersection;
- transform valley or ridge expression;
- inherited scar later modified by sediment or volcanism.

## 3. Continental strike-slip systems

Continental transforms commonly spread deformation across multiple fault strands rather than one perfectly sharp line.

Possible architecture:

- principal displacement zone;
- parallel and branching fault strands;
- stepovers;
- releasing and restraining bends;
- uplifted blocks;
- sedimentary basins;
- local volcanism or geothermal activity in transtensional regions;
- offset rivers, fans, ridges, and coastlines;
- fault-zone valleys created by weakened rock and erosion.

The visible plate boundary may therefore be tens to hundreds of kilometers wide in some settings, while individual active strands remain narrow.

## 4. Releasing bends and pull-apart basins

A releasing bend or compatible stepover creates local extension within an overall strike-slip system.

Potential consequences:

- rhombic, sigmoidal, or elongate basin geometry;
- steep basin-bounding faults;
- rapid localized subsidence;
- thick sediment accumulation;
- lakes or marine basins;
- volcanism or high heat flow in some settings;
- linkage between overlapping fault strands.

The basin shape must derive from fault slip sense and stepover geometry.

Procedural failure:

- placing a generic oval basin at random along every transform;
- generating a circular depression disconnected from the fault network;
- ignoring sediment fill and drainage capture.

## 5. Restraining bends and pressure ridges

A restraining bend or incompatible stepover creates local compression.

Potential consequences:

- uplifted pressure ridges;
- thrust and reverse faults;
- folded sedimentary rocks;
- narrow transpressional mountain belts;
- uplifted blocks and basins on opposite sides;
- deflected drainage;
- local exhumation.

A transform system can therefore create elongated mountains without continent–continent collision.

Future audit implication:

> Mountain-belt diagnosis must check the declared kinematics and fault geometry before labeling all linear uplift as collisional orogeny.

## 6. Transpression

Transpression combines strike-slip and shortening.

Possible architecture:

- oblique-slip faults;
- strike-slip strands plus thrusts;
- positive flower structures;
- en echelon folds;
- narrow uplifted belts;
- crustal thickening distributed asymmetrically;
- rotating blocks;
- partitioned motion between faults.

The relative importance of shear and shortening can vary along strike and through time.

## 7. Transtension

Transtension combines strike-slip and extension.

Possible architecture:

- strike-slip faults plus normal faults;
- negative flower structures;
- pull-apart basins;
- oblique rifts;
- localized crustal thinning;
- fault-controlled volcanic centers;
- marine gateways or lakes;
- segmented subsidence.

Transtensional rifts must not be treated as pure orthogonal continental rifts.

## 8. Slip partitioning

Oblique plate motion may partition into separate structures:

- one fault system carries most strike-slip motion;
- another carries shortening or extension;
- forearc slivers move parallel to trenches;
- blocks rotate between fault systems;
- strain localization migrates.

The final surface may show multiple parallel belts with different structural roles.

Unsafe generator behavior:

- applying the total plate-motion vector identically to every nearby fault;
- creating duplicate parallel terrain features with no partitioned function;
- assigning a single boundary type to an entire broad zone.

## 9. Diffuse plate boundaries

The rigid-plate approximation is useful but incomplete. Some relative motion is distributed across broad zones rather than concentrated on one line.

Diffuse boundaries may involve:

- many small faults;
- distributed shortening or extension;
- intraplate seismicity;
- broad folding;
- block rotation;
- microplate formation;
- reactivation of inherited structures;
- low strain rates integrated over large areas and long times.

Gordon's work on plate non-rigidity and diffuse boundaries shows why plate models require zones as well as lines. Bird's PB2002 model similarly represents some broad boundary regions rather than reducing all plate motion to infinitely thin traces.

WorldWright consequence:

> A diffuse boundary should produce distributed causal features with gradients in strain, not a blurred copy of a sharp boundary mask.

## 10. Microplates and slivers

Microplates may form where:

- major boundaries intersect;
- rifts propagate;
- arcs move independently;
- strike-slip systems partition motion;
- spreading centers reorganize;
- diffuse strain localizes into new boundaries.

They can create:

- short ridge and trench segments;
- rotating blocks;
- complex triple junctions;
- overlapping active and abandoned structures;
- rapid changes in boundary type over short distances.

The complexity must remain kinematically closed: motion around the microplate should be mutually compatible.

## 11. Triple junctions

Triple junctions connect three plate boundaries.

Possible combinations include ridge–ridge–ridge, ridge–ridge–transform, trench–trench–transform, and many others.

Important requirements:

- boundary orientations and motions must be kinematically compatible;
- junctions may migrate;
- branches may be stable, unstable, active, or abandoned;
- nearby deformation can be broader than ordinary two-plate boundaries;
- failed rifts or extinct ridges may preserve former junction positions.

Procedural failure:

- simply meeting three random lines at a point;
- producing a perfectly symmetric three-armed star regardless of plate motions;
- failing to distinguish active and failed arms.

## 12. Fault segmentation and linkage

Strike-slip faults evolve through:

- nucleation of separate segments;
- overlap and relay zones;
- linkage into larger throughgoing faults;
- abandonment of strands;
- transfer of slip;
- earthquake-cycle rupture segmentation;
- long-term geometric smoothing or increased complexity.

Topography may preserve both active and abandoned segments.

Stage 2 must eventually distinguish geological fault segmentation from procedural polyline noise.

## 13. Drainage and sediment response

Strike-slip systems may:

- offset river channels;
- create shutter ridges;
- dam valleys;
- divert drainage along fault zones;
- capture rivers into pull-apart basins;
- generate alluvial fans at basin margins;
- produce axial lakes or marine basins;
- preserve offset terraces and fans.

A transform system with unchanged drainage is incomplete, especially at regional and local scales.

## 14. Temporal evolution

Possible sequence:

1. distributed shear or inherited weakness;
2. fault-segment nucleation;
3. linkage and localization;
4. development of pull-aparts or pressure ridges;
5. slip partitioning and block rotation;
6. boundary migration or microplate formation;
7. abandonment or inversion;
8. erosion and sediment burial of fossil strands.

A fossil transform should retain offset or basin inheritance without appearing everywhere as a fresh active scar.

## 15. Multiscale visual obligations

### Planetary scale

- transform boundaries connect compatible spreading, convergent, or diffuse systems;
- fracture zones preserve past oceanic plate motion;
- broad diffuse zones occupy plausible plate-interior regions;
- triple junctions close kinematically.

### Continental scale

- fault-zone width and strand organization;
- pull-apart and transpressional basin/mountain distribution;
- block rotation and microplates;
- drainage offsets and sediment routing.

### Regional scale

- stepovers and bends;
- pressure ridges;
- rhombic basins;
- flower structures;
- en echelon folds and faults;
- fault-controlled volcanic or geothermal areas.

### Local scale

- offset streams;
- scarps;
- shutter ridges;
- sag ponds;
- fault gouge valleys;
- displaced fans and terraces;
- local landslides.

## 16. Threshold and interaction axes

Future Stage 2 cases must cross:

- slip rate;
- fault-zone width;
- plate-motion obliquity;
- stepover length and sense;
- crust type and thickness;
- inherited weakness;
- sediment supply;
- climate and erosion;
- localization strength;
- block size and rotation;
- active versus fossil state;
- connection to ridges, trenches, or collision zones.

No universal numeric bins are approved yet.

## 18. Procedural failure signatures

### 18.1 Straight-line scar
Every transform is a uniform straight groove regardless of crust or history.

### 18.2 Random pull-aparts
Oval basins are placed without compatible stepover geometry.

### 18.3 Wrong bend response
Releasing bends uplift and restraining bends subside, or slip sense is ignored.

### 18.4 Diffuse equals blurred
A broad boundary is created by Gaussian blur around a sharp line rather than distributed structures.

### 18.5 Kinematically impossible microplate
Boundary motions around a block do not close.

### 18.6 Symmetric triple-junction star
All three arms have identical length, type, and activity.

### 18.7 Every strand active
Inherited and abandoned faults remain equally visible and influential.

### 18.8 No drainage offset
Large cumulative strike-slip motion leaves rivers and landforms unmodified.

### 18.9 Transform-only flatness
The generator assumes strike-slip systems cannot create mountains or basins.

## 19. Stage 2 coverage obligations

Future generation must eventually include:

- narrow oceanic transform;
- fracture-zone inheritance;
- continental single-strand transform;
- broad multi-strand transform system;
- releasing bend and pull-apart basin;
- restraining bend and pressure ridge;
- transpressional uplift belt;
- transtensional oblique rift;
- slip-partitioned forearc or continental system;
- diffuse shortening and diffuse extension zones;
- microplate and rotating-block cases;
- stable and migrating triple junctions;
- active, abandoned, buried, and inverted states.

### Negative controls

- uniform straight groove;
- random oval pull-apart;
- bend response inconsistent with slip sense;
- blurred diffuse boundary;
- impossible microplate closure;
- symmetric triple-junction star;
- all strands active;
- no drainage offset;
- transforms forced to remain topographically flat.

## 21. Conclusions safe enough to carry forward

### High confidence

- transform systems can generate both uplift and subsidence through bends, stepovers, and obliquity;
- pull-apart and pressure-ridge geometry must follow slip sense;
- continental transforms may be broad multi-strand zones;
- diffuse boundaries are not blurred sharp boundaries;
- microplate and triple-junction geometry must be kinematically compatible;
- active and inherited fault strands require separate states;
- drainage and sediment systems preserve cumulative strike-slip effects.

### Model-dependent or incomplete

- quantitative fault-zone width rules;
- block-rotation thresholds;
- long-term localization of diffuse zones;
- cross-planet strike-slip morphology;
- precise triple-junction stability criteria for procedural generation.

### Not approved

- generic transform grooves;
- random pull-apart placement;
- blurred diffuse masks;
- numeric scoring thresholds;
- image generation.
