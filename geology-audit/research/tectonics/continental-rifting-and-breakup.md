# Continental rifting and breakup

## Research status

- **Domain IDs:** T02, C03, O01, linked to F03–F05, V01–V03, M02–M03, X03
- **Status:** IN RESEARCH
- **Stage 2 generation:** BLOCKED
- **Purpose:** define how continental lithosphere extends, localizes, subsides, magmatically modifies, and may progress to oceanic spreading.

## 1. Central rule

A continental rift is not simply a valley drawn through a continent.

It is an evolving deformation system that may involve:

- distributed extension;
- fault localization;
- crustal thinning;
- lithospheric thinning;
- mantle upwelling;
- magmatism or magma starvation;
- uplift of rift shoulders;
- subsidence and sediment accumulation;
- drainage disruption;
- segmentation and linkage;
- oblique extension;
- failed branches;
- eventual breakup or abandonment.

The visible rift, its flanks, sedimentary basins, magmatism, thermal state, and continent–ocean transition must remain causally linked.

## 2. Rift evolution is staged

A generalized sequence may include:

1. pre-rift inherited lithosphere;
2. distributed extension and fault nucleation;
3. localization into linked fault systems;
4. basin growth and sediment accumulation;
5. crustal and lithospheric hyperextension;
6. mantle exhumation and/or major magmatism;
7. breakup and onset of seafloor spreading;
8. post-rift thermal subsidence;
9. passive-margin maturation.

Not every rift completes this sequence. Some fail, migrate, reverse, or are overprinted.

## 3. Narrow and wide rifts

### Narrow-rift tendency

- deformation localizes into a comparatively restricted zone;
- major border faults and deep asymmetric basins may dominate;
- rift shoulders may be strong and sharply expressed;
- crust and mantle deformation may be mechanically coupled;
- segment linkage creates an axial basin chain rather than one uniform trench.

### Wide-rift tendency

- deformation is distributed over a broader area;
- multiple fault systems and basins may coexist;
- lower-crustal flow or crust–mantle decoupling may broaden extension;
- long-wavelength uplift and subsidence may be more diffuse;
- a single central valley may be absent.

WorldWright consequence:

> Rift width cannot be derived from extension magnitude alone. Thermal state, rheology, inheritance, strain rate, and crust–mantle coupling must be represented.

## 4. Rift migration and margin asymmetry

Brune et al. modeled hyperextended margins as products of migrating deformation, sequential faulting, lower-crustal flow, and changing lithospheric strength. Their results show that conjugate margins can become strongly asymmetric even when they formed as one rift system.

Important visual consequences:

- fault activity can migrate oceanward;
- older proximal faults become inactive while new distal faults form;
- one margin may be very wide and hyperextended while the conjugate is narrow;
- preserved pre-breakup sediment packages may remain relatively undeformed landward;
- crustal material can be transferred across the evolving rift system;
- the final continent–ocean boundary need not sit at the center of the original rift.

Unsafe generator behavior:

- forcing mirror symmetry across every new ocean basin;
- keeping all faults active simultaneously;
- centering the coastline on the original rift axis;
- representing rift migration as a blurred widening of one trench.

## 5. Magma-poor margins

Magma-poor rifting may involve:

- severe crustal thinning;
- large detachment faults;
- hyperextended continental slivers;
- exhumed or serpentinized mantle;
- sparse syn-rift volcanism;
- delayed transition to ordinary oceanic crust;
- strong conjugate asymmetry.

The key visual identity is not “no volcanoes.” It is extension accommodated substantially by faulting, thinning, and possible mantle exhumation rather than thick volcanic construction.

## 6. Magma-rich margins

Magma-rich or volcanic rifted margins may involve:

- large volumes of intrusive and extrusive magma;
- underplating;
- thickened igneous crust;
- seaward-dipping volcanic sequences;
- broad volcanic plateaus;
- dike swarms;
- early flood-basalt provinces;
- high heat flow and uplift before or during breakup.

Important caution:

A volcanic margin is not simply a passive margin with random volcanoes. Magmatism modifies crustal thickness, rheology, uplift, subsidence, and the continent–ocean transition.

## 7. Intermediate margins

Recent work emphasizes that some margins combine traits of classic magma-rich and magma-poor end members.

Potential mixed behavior:

- detachment faulting plus significant intrusions;
- magmatic underplating without large seaward-dipping reflectors;
- eventual normal-thickness oceanic crust;
- thermal inheritance from earlier subduction or magmatism;
- changing magmatic supply through time.

Stage 1 implication:

> The audit must not force every rifted margin into a binary volcanic/non-volcanic classification.

## 8. Segmentation and obliquity

Rifts are commonly segmented and oblique to the regional extension direction.

Potential effects:

- en echelon basins;
- transfer zones;
- accommodation zones;
- relay ramps;
- strike-slip components;
- rotating fault blocks;
- variable shoulder uplift;
- offset volcanic centers;
- changing segment polarity.

The along-strike geometry can inherit older crustal provinces and faults.

Procedural failure:

- equal-length parallel grabens repeated at regular intervals;
- all segments connected by identical cross faults;
- rift axis following a smooth noise line without mechanical inheritance.

## 9. Rift shoulders and uplift

Rift flanks may rise due to combinations of:

- flexural response to faulting and unloading;
- thermal buoyancy;
- dynamic mantle support;
- isostatic response to erosion;
- magmatic underplating;
- fault-footwall uplift.

A rift shoulder is not guaranteed, and its amplitude may differ strongly between sides.

WorldWright consequence:

- shoulder uplift requires a declared support mechanism;
- shoulder shape should follow segment and fault geometry;
- shoulder erosion and drainage should modify the initial uplift.

## 10. Rift basins and sediment

Rift basins are accommodation systems, not empty depressions.

They may contain:

- alluvial fans;
- lakes;
- rivers;
- evaporites;
- volcanic deposits;
- marine incursions;
- growth strata thickening toward active faults;
- axial and transverse sediment routing.

Sediment supply and water balance can make the final surface appear flat even where the structural basin is deep.

Required separation:

- structural basement depth;
- sediment thickness;
- water depth;
- surface elevation;
- active-fault geometry.

## 11. Drainage response

Rifting may:

- split pre-existing drainage basins;
- capture rivers along the rift axis;
- create internally drained lakes;
- route transverse rivers across shoulders;
- generate waterfalls and knickpoints;
- reverse drainage during shoulder uplift or basin linkage;
- eventually create marine gateways.

A final rift map with rivers unaffected by faults, shoulders, or basin subsidence is causally incomplete.

## 12. Failed rifts and aulacogens

Some rifts do not reach breakup.

Possible fossil expressions:

- elongated sedimentary basins;
- inherited fault zones;
- volcanic remnants;
- weakened crust later reactivated;
- drainage corridors;
- subsiding intracontinental troughs;
- branches connected to successful ocean basins.

Failed rifts should not remain eternally as fresh active valleys. Their appearance depends on burial, erosion, inversion, and reactivation.

## 13. Breakup and continent–ocean transition

The transition from continental extension to seafloor spreading may involve:

- abrupt or gradual crustal change;
- hyperextended crust;
- exhumed mantle;
- transitional magmatic crust;
- localized first oceanic accretion;
- ridge jumps;
- asymmetric conjugate structures.

Stage 1 rule:

> The continent–ocean transition is a geological zone, not a coastline-distance ring.

The shoreline may lie far landward or seaward of the crustal transition depending on sediment, sea level, subsidence, uplift, and erosion.

## 14. Post-rift thermal subsidence

After active extension ends:

- lithosphere cools;
- basin subsidence may continue;
- sediment accumulates;
- shelves and slopes develop;
- faults become buried or reactivated;
- drainage systems reorganize;
- margins may remain asymmetric.

The passive-margin stage must preserve rift inheritance without making every old fault visible at the surface.

## 15. Multiscale visual obligations

### Planetary scale

- rifts connect coherently to plate motion and potential breakup geometry;
- successful rifts transition to ocean basins and spreading centers;
- failed branches remain as inherited continental structures;
- conjugate margins preserve compatible but not necessarily symmetric histories.

### Continental scale

- segmented basin chains;
- variable rift width;
- shoulder uplift;
- volcanic provinces where appropriate;
- drainage and sediment routing;
- branch competition.

### Regional scale

- half-grabens and grabens;
- border faults;
- transfer zones;
- relay ramps;
- lakes and sediment wedges;
- detachment systems;
- hyperextended crust.

### Local scale

- scarps;
- fault facets;
- alluvial fans;
- fissures;
- dikes and volcanic centers;
- growth strata;
- shoreline and delta response.

## 16. Threshold and interaction axes

Future Stage 2 cases must cross:

- extension magnitude;
- extension rate;
- extension obliquity;
- lithosphere thickness;
- lower-crust rheology;
- inherited weakness;
- magma supply;
- mantle temperature;
- sediment supply;
- surface water and climate;
- active versus failed versus post-rift state;
- sea-level history.

No universal numeric bins are approved yet.

## 17. Required audit metadata

- rift state: distributed, localized, hyperextended, breakup, post-rift, failed, inverted;
- width class;
- extension direction and obliquity;
- strain-localization strength;
- fault polarity and segmentation;
- crust–mantle coupling class;
- lower-crust rheology class;
- magma-poor, intermediate, or magma-rich branch;
- rift-migration state;
- shoulder-support mechanism;
- sediment-fill state;
- drainage state;
- continent–ocean-transition confidence;
- inheritance source;
- active versus fossil expression.

## 18. Procedural failure signatures

### 18.1 Single uniform trench
Every continental rift is rendered as one constant-width depression.

### 18.2 Perfect conjugate symmetry
Margins mirror each other despite migration, rheology, and asymmetric faulting.

### 18.3 Distance-to-axis terrain
Shoulders, basin depth, volcanism, and crustal thinning are all generated from one radial or linear falloff.

### 18.4 Simultaneous fault activation
All faults remain equally active instead of migrating, linking, abandoning, or localizing.

### 18.5 Volcanism sprinkled independently
Volcanoes ignore the thermal and structural rift system.

### 18.6 Coastline equals crust boundary
The visible coast is used as the continent–ocean transition.

### 18.7 Empty basin
Structural lows remain unfilled despite high sediment supply and long duration.

### 18.8 Failed rift frozen in youth
Ancient aulacogens retain fresh scarps and open valleys without burial or erosion.

## 19. Stage 2 coverage obligations

Future generation must eventually include:

- narrow active rift;
- wide distributed rift;
- oblique segmented rift;
- magma-poor hyperextension;
- magma-rich volcanic margin;
- intermediate mixed margin;
- migrating asymmetric rift;
- successful breakup sequence;
- failed rift sequence;
- triple-junction branch competition;
- sediment-rich and sediment-starved rifts;
- arid, humid, lacustrine, and marine-inundated states;
- post-rift thermal subsidence and passive-margin inheritance.

### Negative controls

- uniform rift trench;
- mirrored conjugate margins;
- axis-distance shoulder rings;
- all faults active at once;
- random rift volcanism;
- coastline used as crustal boundary;
- unfilled ancient basin;
- failed rift preserved as fresh active terrain.

## 21. Conclusions safe enough to carry forward

### High confidence

- rifts evolve through stages and may fail before breakup;
- width, symmetry, and fault pattern depend on rheology, thermal state, inheritance, and extension history;
- magma-poor, magma-rich, and intermediate margins require separate branches;
- rift migration can produce strongly asymmetric conjugate margins;
- sediment and drainage are integral to visible rift morphology;
- the continent–ocean transition is not equivalent to the shoreline.

### Model-dependent or incomplete

- exact rift-migration controls;
- universal width thresholds;
- quantitative magma-supply boundaries;
- cross-planet rift behavior;
- long-term persistence of inherited structures.

### Not approved

- universal rift templates;
- mirrored margins;
- numeric scoring thresholds;
- image generation.
