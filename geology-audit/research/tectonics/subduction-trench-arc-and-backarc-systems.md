# Subduction, trench, arc, forearc, and backarc systems

## Research status

- **Domain IDs:** T03, T04, O02, O05, M01, linked to V02–V03, C01–C03, X03
- **Status:** IN RESEARCH
- **Stage 2 generation:** BLOCKED
- **Purpose:** define the coupled geometry and variable surface expression of ocean–continent and ocean–ocean subduction systems.

## 1. Central rule

A subduction zone is not merely a trench plus a parallel volcano line.

It is a coupled system containing some combination of:

- downgoing oceanic lithosphere;
- trench or sediment-filled trough;
- outer rise and bending faults;
- accretionary wedge or erosive margin;
- forearc high and forearc basin;
- megathrust interface;
- mantle wedge;
- volcanic arc;
- crustal plutonic roots;
- backarc compression, stability, or extension;
- slab rollback, advance, flattening, tearing, or windows;
- earthquake and deformation zones;
- sediment and volatile transfer.

The trench, forearc, arc, backarc, slab geometry, overriding-plate deformation, and sediment budget must remain causally linked.

## 2. Subducting plate requirements

Stable modern-style subduction generally involves negatively buoyant oceanic lithosphere, but actual behavior depends on:

- plate age and thermal state;
- crustal and sediment thickness;
- seamounts, ridges, plateaus, and fracture zones;
- convergence rate and obliquity;
- slab pull and resistance;
- overriding-plate motion;
- mantle viscosity;
- inherited plate-boundary geometry;
- hydration and phase changes.

WorldWright must not select slab dip, trench depth, or arc strength from plate type alone.

## 3. Trench and outer-rise morphology

### 3.1 Trench

A trench is the surface depression associated with bending and descent of the subducting plate, but its bathymetric visibility varies strongly.

It may be:

- narrow and very deep;
- broad and sediment-filled;
- partly obscured by an accretionary prism;
- segmented by subducting features;
- laterally variable along one margin;
- offset by tears, transforms, or oblique convergence.

A weakly expressed trench does not mean subduction is absent.

### 3.2 Outer rise

Before entering the trench, the downgoing plate bends upward and downward, producing:

- a broad outer rise;
- normal faulting;
- increased permeability and hydration;
- seafloor roughness;
- local sediment disruption.

Stage 2 should eventually include outer-rise expression where resolution permits, but it should not become a universal visible ridge.

## 4. Accretionary versus erosive margins

### Accretionary tendency

- sediment and slices of incoming crust are transferred to the overriding plate;
- a wedge or prism grows;
- trench fill may be substantial;
- forearc width and topography may increase;
- fold-and-thrust structures may parallel the margin;
- forearc basins may form landward of the wedge.

### Erosive tendency

- material is removed from the underside or front of the overriding plate;
- the forearc may narrow or subside;
- trenchward migration of the coast or arc may occur;
- crustal loss can accompany long-term subduction;
- a deep trench can coexist with a relatively steep margin.

The two styles may vary along strike and through time.

Unsafe generator behavior:

- assigning every subduction margin the same prism width;
- using sediment amount only to color the trench;
- treating accretion/erosion as cosmetic rather than changing forearc architecture.

## 5. Forearc system

The forearc lies between the trench and volcanic arc.

Potential elements:

- accretionary prism or frontal wedge;
- outer-arc high;
- forearc basin;
- uplifted or subsiding coastal blocks;
- serpentinite or volcanic features in some oceanic forearcs;
- megathrust-related deformation;
- sediment routing parallel and transverse to the margin.

Forearc topography reflects a balance among:

- plate-interface coupling;
- wedge mechanics;
- sediment input;
- buoyancy;
- tectonic erosion/accretion;
- slab geometry;
- inherited overriding-plate structure.

WorldWright consequence:

> The forearc cannot be represented as a fixed-width empty strip between a trench and volcano chain.

## 6. Volcanic arc position and width

Arc magmatism is associated with mantle-wedge melting triggered and focused by slab-derived volatiles, thermal structure, mantle flow, and melt transport.

The arc is usually broadly parallel to the trench but can vary in:

- distance from trench;
- width;
- continuity;
- volcano spacing;
- volume;
- composition;
- elevation;
- plutonic versus volcanic expression;
- along-arc gaps and clusters.

England and Katz proposed a model in which the position of arcs is controlled by where the mantle wedge crosses the anhydrous solidus, focusing melt pathways. Later thermal models also show that magmatic heat transport can materially alter subarc temperatures. These are important process branches, not grounds for a universal fixed trench-to-arc distance.

## 7. Ocean–continent subduction

Potential surface architecture:

- ocean trench or sedimented trough;
- continental forearc;
- coastal or inland volcanic arc;
- batholiths and crustal thickening;
- foreland or retroarc basins;
- broad shortening in the overriding continent;
- plateau development in some end members;
- narrow or broad coastal mountains;
- flat-slab segments with volcanic gaps.

Important variation controls:

- slab dip;
- plate coupling;
- convergence obliquity;
- upper-plate motion;
- incoming plate age and buoyancy;
- sediment supply;
- continental crust thickness;
- subducting ridges or plateaus;
- rollback or advance.

## 8. Ocean–ocean subduction

Potential surface architecture:

- deep trench;
- oceanic forearc;
- island arc;
- remnant arcs;
- backarc basin or spreading center;
- arc curvature and migration;
- submarine volcanic chains;
- localized emergent islands.

Ocean–ocean systems can be highly arcuate and may migrate through slab rollback. A curved island arc is therefore not a radial blob merely because it wraps around an ocean basin.

## 9. Slab dip and flat-slab behavior

Slab geometry changes the whole surface system.

### Steeper-slab tendency

- narrower zone of overriding-plate interaction in some settings;
- arc closer to the trench relative to shallower slabs, though no single distance rule applies;
- stronger rollback/backarc-extension potential in some systems;
- pronounced trench and oceanic forearc end members.

### Shallow or flat-slab tendency

- volcanic arc migration inland or shutdown over the flat segment;
- deformation transmitted far into the overriding plate;
- broad crustal shortening;
- stronger coupling;
- possible plateau/ridge subduction influence;
- segmented along-strike behavior where slab geometry changes.

Martinod et al. emphasize the role of buoyant oceanic plateaus in creating flat-slab segments and shifting deformation far inland in the Andes.

## 10. Backarc states

The backarc is not always extensional.

Possible states:

- stable;
- compressional;
- transtensional;
- rifting;
- active seafloor spreading;
- extinct or remnant basin.

Controls include:

- trench rollback or advance;
- overriding-plate motion;
- slab dip and sinking rate;
- mantle-wedge flow;
- inherited lithosphere;
- collision at arc ends;
- changes in convergence.

Stage 2 must not automatically place a backarc basin behind every arc.

## 11. Slab rollback, advance, tearing, and windows

### Rollback

Trench retreat can:

- extend the overriding plate;
- migrate the arc;
- open backarc basins;
- curve or lengthen arc systems;
- isolate remnant arcs.

### Advance

Trench advance can:

- increase compression in the overriding plate;
- narrow or close backarc basins;
- strengthen coupling with continental shortening.

### Slab tear or window

A tear, ridge collision, or slab window may produce:

- volcanic gaps;
- unusual magmatism;
- localized uplift or subsidence;
- abrupt along-arc changes;
- mantle-flow anomalies;
- reorganization of deformation.

Adam et al. found that subducting oceanic plateaus, hotspot chains, ridges, and spreading centers correlate with varied arc-volcanism responses, including both increases and gaps. This reinforces that incoming-plate heterogeneity must remain explicit.

## 12. Sediment budget

Sediment influences:

- trench depth and visibility;
- accretionary-wedge growth;
- megathrust properties;
- forearc-basin fill;
- tectonic erosion/accretion balance;
- fluid transport;
- coastal morphology.

High sediment supply can make a subduction margin look less trench-like at the surface, while still preserving the deep plate geometry.

Required separation:

- structural trench depth;
- sediment thickness;
- water depth;
- wedge geometry;
- final bathymetry.

## 13. Oblique convergence and partitioning

When convergence is oblique, motion may partition into:

- trench-normal shortening;
- arc-parallel strike-slip faulting;
- forearc sliver motion;
- rotating blocks;
- localized transpression or transtension;
- pull-apart basins within the arc or forearc.

The system may therefore contain both convergent and transform-like features without becoming geologically incoherent.

## 14. Temporal evolution

A subduction system can evolve through:

- initiation;
- immature trench/forearc development;
- mature arc construction;
- rollback and backarc opening;
- collision of ridges, plateaus, or continents;
- slab flattening;
- arc migration or shutdown;
- slab tearing or breakoff;
- polarity reversal or termination;
- fossil arc and suture preservation.

Stage 2 must eventually include active, waning, fossil, and interrupted states.

## 15. Multiscale visual obligations

### Planetary scale

- connected convergent-margin network compatible with plate motions;
- trenches linked to consuming oceanic lithosphere;
- arc and backarc systems arranged on the overriding plate;
- crust-age termination at subduction zones;
- major along-strike segmentation.

### Margin scale

- trench/outer-rise system;
- wedge or erosive forearc;
- volcanic/plutonic arc;
- backarc state;
- upper-plate deformation;
- sediment routing.

### Regional scale

- forearc basins;
- accretionary ridges;
- volcanic clusters and gaps;
- flat-slab inland deformation;
- subducting-ridge disruptions;
- remnant arcs and backarc spreading centers.

### Local scale

- thrusts and folds;
- volcanic edifices and calderas;
- trench-slope channels;
- uplifted marine terraces;
- faulted forearc blocks;
- landslides and sediment fans.

## 18. Procedural failure signatures

### 18.1 Universal trench–arc spacing
Every subduction zone receives the same parallel offset regardless of slab geometry or upper-plate structure.

### 18.2 Identical trench depth
Sediment-rich and sediment-starved margins have the same bathymetric trench.

### 18.3 Arc as dotted pearls
Volcanoes are equally sized and evenly spaced with no clusters, gaps, crustal roots, or along-arc variability.

### 18.4 Automatic backarc basin
Every arc receives a parallel extensional basin.

### 18.5 Plate boundary painted into terrain
The trench, wedge, arc, and backarc are all derived from one distance field.

### 18.6 Flat slab with unchanged arc
Slab flattening does not migrate or suppress volcanism and does not transmit deformation inland.

### 18.7 Incoming features ignored
Subducting plateaus, ridges, fracture zones, and seamount chains have no along-margin effect.

### 18.8 Forearc omitted
Trench connects directly to volcanoes with no wedge, forearc, or coastal architecture.

### 18.9 Obliquity ignored
Oblique convergence produces no strike-slip partitioning or forearc motion.

## 19. Stage 2 coverage obligations

Future generation must eventually include:

- sediment-starved ocean–ocean subduction;
- sediment-rich ocean–continent subduction;
- accretionary and erosive margins;
- steep-, moderate-, shallow-, and flat-slab branches;
- rollback with backarc spreading;
- compressional backarc;
- volcanic arc clusters and gaps;
- subducting ridge/plateau/fracture-zone disruptions;
- oblique convergence and forearc slivers;
- active, waning, fossil, and interrupted arcs;
- trench partly or fully obscured by sediment;
- remnant arcs and extinct backarc basins.

### Negative controls

- fixed trench–arc offset;
- identical trenches;
- evenly spaced arc pearls;
- universal backarc basin;
- one distance-field system;
- unchanged arc over flat slab;
- ignored incoming features;
- missing forearc;
- no obliquity partitioning.

## 21. Conclusions safe enough to carry forward

### High confidence

- subduction zones are coupled trench–forearc–arc–backarc systems;
- trench visibility depends strongly on sediment and margin style;
- accretionary and erosive margins require different forearc evolution;
- slab geometry and upper-plate motion affect arc and backarc behavior;
- flat-slab segments can shift deformation inland and suppress/migrate volcanism;
- oblique convergence partitions strain;
- incoming plate heterogeneity creates along-arc variation.

### Model-dependent or incomplete

- exact arc-position mechanism;
- universal slab-dip thresholds;
- quantitative coupling-to-topography relations;
- subduction initiation on other planets;
- amplitude of dynamic support around slabs.

### Not approved

- universal trench/arc templates;
- fixed offsets;
- numeric scoring thresholds;
- image generation.
