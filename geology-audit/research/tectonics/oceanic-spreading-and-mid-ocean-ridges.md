# Oceanic spreading and mid-ocean-ridge systems

## Research status

- **Domain ID:** T01, with links to C02, O04, O05, T06, X03
- **Status:** IN RESEARCH
- **Stage 2 generation:** BLOCKED
- **Purpose:** define how new oceanic lithosphere is created, segmented, cooled, faulted, and recorded in bathymetry.

## 1. Central rule

A spreading ridge is not simply a raised line drawn through an ocean basin.

It is a coupled system involving:

- plate separation;
- mantle upwelling;
- decompression melting;
- crustal accretion;
- normal faulting;
- segmentation;
- transforms or overlapping spreading centers;
- hydrothermal circulation;
- cooling and subsidence away from the axis;
- formation of abyssal hills and fracture zones.

The ridge axis, ridge flanks, transform offsets, crustal age, heat flow, and basin depth must remain causally linked.

## 2. Spreading rate is important but not sufficient

Spreading rate strongly affects ridge morphology, but it does not act alone.

Other controlling variables include:

- mantle temperature and composition;
- magma supply;
- lithosphere thickness;
- extension obliquity;
- ridge segmentation;
- inherited structures;
- nearby hotspots or plumes;
- water depth and pressure;
- transform spacing;
- hydrothermal cooling;
- temporal variability in accretion.

Stage 1 should therefore treat slow, intermediate, fast, and ultraslow ridges as process families, not as fixed height presets.

## 3. Fast-spreading ridge tendency

Fast-spreading ridges commonly have:

- comparatively continuous magmatic accretion;
- an axial high or only shallow axial depression rather than a deep rift valley;
- smoother ridge-flank topography at broad scale;
- overlapping spreading centers and small offsets in some segments;
- abundant sheeted and volcanic crustal construction;
- abyssal-hill fabrics generated by faulting and plate unbending.

Important caution:

A fast ridge is not perfectly smooth. Fine-scale faulting, volcanic construction, segmentation, and temporal magma-supply variation remain visible.

## 4. Slow-spreading ridge tendency

Slow-spreading ridges commonly have:

- a pronounced axial valley;
- stronger tectonic extension relative to magmatic filling;
- rugged fault-block topography;
- larger and more persistent normal faults;
- greater along-axis variability;
- transform-bounded segments;
- exposure of deeper crustal or mantle rocks in some locations.

The morphological identity is therefore not merely “lower ridge.” It is a stronger tectonic-versus-magmatic partitioning.

## 5. Ultraslow spreading

Dick, Lin, and Schouten identified ultraslow ridges as a distinct class rather than merely the slow end of a continuous ridge spectrum.

Important tendencies include:

- very low melt supply in many segments;
- coexistence of magmatic and amagmatic accretion;
- broad regions of mantle exposure or highly tectonic seafloor generation;
- strong segmentation;
- oblique or unusual ridge geometry;
- deep axial regions and large relief;
- local volcanic centers separated by melt-poor zones.

WorldWright consequence:

> Low spreading rate cannot be represented by uniformly reducing the height or magma intensity of an otherwise fast-ridge template.

## 6. Detachment faults and oceanic core complexes

At some slow and ultraslow ridges, long-lived detachment faults can accommodate a large share of extension.

Potential surface expressions:

- broad faulted domes;
- corrugated or striated fault surfaces;
- asymmetric ridge-flank architecture;
- exposure of lower crust or mantle;
- displaced volcanic accretion toward the opposite side;
- oceanic core complexes near segment ends or ridge-transform intersections.

These are valid large local domes and must not be confused with continent-scale procedural radial blobs.

## 7. Segmentation

Ridges are divided into segments at multiple scales.

Possible segment boundaries include:

- transform faults;
- non-transform discontinuities;
- overlapping spreading centers;
- propagating rifts;
- changes in magma supply;
- oblique offsets;
- triple junctions.

Along-axis morphology may change between segment centers and ends because magma supply, faulting, and thermal structure vary.

Future audit obligation:

- a ridge should show connected global logic;
- local segmentation should be allowed;
- segmentation should not become regular tiling or identical repeated modules.

## 8. Transform faults and fracture zones

Active transform faults offset ridge axes and accommodate strike-slip motion between spreading segments.

Inactive continuations become fracture zones that preserve past plate-motion geometry across older seafloor.

Important distinctions:

- active transform segment: present plate boundary and seismic deformation;
- inactive fracture zone: inherited scar within one plate;
- ridge-transform intersection: complex thermal, tectonic, and magmatic transition;
- fracture-zone relief: can persist because crust of different age and thermal structure is juxtaposed.

Unsafe generator behavior:

- treating every fracture zone as an active fault;
- making transforms perpendicular by geometric rule regardless of plate motion;
- drawing uniform trenches along all transform offsets;
- failing to continue fracture-zone flow lines into older crust.

## 9. Crustal age and basin depth

New oceanic lithosphere is hot and buoyant near the spreading axis. As it moves away:

- it cools;
- the thermal boundary layer thickens;
- density increases;
- broad seafloor depth increases;
- sediment tends to accumulate with age and proximity to sources;
- local departures arise from swells, plateaus, trenches, fracture zones, seamounts, flexure, and dynamic topography.

WorldWright consequence:

> Crust age should influence thermal subsidence through a physical relation, but it must not be copied directly into a smooth bathymetric gradient without local geology.

## 10. Abyssal hills

Abyssal hills are a widespread coherent fabric of ocean-floor topography associated with faulting and volcanic construction near spreading centers.

Their orientation and spacing may reflect:

- ridge-axis orientation;
- fault spacing;
- spreading rate;
- elastic plate structure;
- magma supply;
- temporal forcing;
- off-axis deformation.

The 2026 Katz and Huybers model proposes that small plate-thickness variations linked to glacial sea-level cycles could pace fault spacing at fast-spreading ridges. This is a model-dependent interaction, not yet a universal Stage 2 rule.

## 11. Hydrothermal and magmatic effects

Hydrothermal circulation changes thermal structure and can influence:

- crustal cooling;
- faulting;
- mineral alteration;
- vent-field distribution;
- local collapse or construction.

Magma transport may be focused or distributed depending on mantle heterogeneity and spreading conditions.

These processes matter for causal completeness, but most should not become directly visible at globe scale.

## 12. Temporal evolution

A ridge system can change through:

- spreading-rate changes;
- propagation or abandonment of segments;
- ridge jumps;
- hotspot interaction;
- changing obliquity;
- transform reorganization;
- magma-supply pulses;
- plate-boundary relocation.

The ocean floor preserves these changes as:

- abandoned ridges;
- extinct spreading centers;
- pseudofaults;
- fracture-zone bends;
- age discontinuities;
- asymmetric crustal sequences.

## 13. Multiscale visual obligations

### Planetary scale

- globally connected ridge network appropriate to the plate system;
- crust-age organization away from active spreading centers;
- links to transforms and triple junctions;
- basin-wide cooling/subsidence pattern.

### Basin scale

- ridge-flank elevation and segmentation;
- transform and fracture-zone networks;
- asymmetry from propagation or ridge jumps;
- volcanic plateaus, swells, and sedimented plains as departures.

### Regional scale

- axial high or rift valley depending on regime;
- fault blocks;
- overlapping centers;
- core complexes;
- segment-center versus segment-end differences.

### Local scale

- fissures;
- lava flows;
- normal faults;
- hydrothermal fields;
- corrugated detachment surfaces;
- abyssal-hill texture.

## 14. Threshold and interaction axes

Future Stage 2 cases must cross:

- spreading rate;
- magma supply;
- mantle temperature;
- extension obliquity;
- segment length and transform spacing;
- crustal accretion continuity;
- lithosphere strength;
- hotspot influence;
- ridge age and abandonment state;
- sediment cover.

No universal numeric bins are approved yet.

## 15. Required audit metadata

- spreading regime: ultraslow, slow, intermediate, fast, uncertain;
- full versus half spreading-rate definition;
- magma-supply class;
- axis morphology;
- segmentation style;
- transform or overlap style;
- obliquity;
- detachment-fault presence;
- crustal age and confidence;
- active, propagating, dying, or extinct state;
- hotspot/plume interaction;
- sediment-cover class;
- expected visible scale.

## 16. Procedural failure signatures

### 16.1 Uniform ridge ribbon
A constant-width elevated stripe is drawn through the ocean with no segmentation, transforms, or age relation.

### 16.2 Rate-as-height shortcut
Spreading rate changes only ridge elevation while faulting and magma partition remain unchanged.

### 16.3 Repeated segment tiles
Every ridge segment has identical length, offset, relief, and volcanic center placement.

### 16.4 Transform trenches
All transforms are rendered as deep linear trenches regardless of active state, crustal age, or relief history.

### 16.5 Age-gradient leakage
Crust age appears as perfectly smooth parallel color or depth bands with no local departures.

### 16.6 Magma-poor ridge with continuous volcanic crest
Ultraslow or melt-poor settings retain an uninterrupted fast-ridge volcanic axis.

### 16.7 Fracture zones detached from plate history
Scars do not align with past spreading geometry or bend when plate motion changes.

## 17. Stage 2 coverage obligations

Future generation must eventually include:

- fast axial-high ridge;
- intermediate ridge;
- slow rift-valley ridge;
- ultraslow mixed magmatic/amagmatic ridge;
- detachment-fault/core-complex segment;
- transform-bounded segments;
- overlapping spreading centers;
- propagating ridge and abandoned pseudofaults;
- hotspot-influenced ridge;
- extinct ridge;
- age-depth and sediment-cover sequences;
- symmetric and asymmetric spreading cases.

### Negative controls

- uniform ridge stripe;
- periodic tiled segments;
- rate-only height scaling;
- transform trench repetition;
- smooth age bands;
- globally continuous magma at an ultraslow ridge;
- fracture zones unrelated to kinematics.

## 19. Conclusions safe enough to carry forward

### High confidence

- ridge morphology depends on tectonic/magmatic partition, not spreading rate alone;
- slow and fast ridges require different axial structures;
- ultraslow ridges can include magmatic and amagmatic accretion;
- transforms, fracture zones, segmentation, and crustal age are coupled to spreading history;
- oceanic core complexes are valid asymmetric domal exceptions;
- cooling and subsidence organize basin-scale depth away from ridges.

### Model-dependent or incomplete

- exact category thresholds;
- sea-level pacing of fault spacing;
- cross-planet ridge morphology;
- quantitative detachment-fault occurrence rules;
- detailed hydrothermal effects.

### Not approved

- uniform ridge templates;
- direct age-to-depth painting;
- numeric scoring thresholds;
- image generation.
