# Karst, dissolution, caves, and subsurface collapse

## Research status

- **Domain IDs:** S09, linked to F06–F07, S01–S05, O01, X01–X04, H01–H06
- **Status:** IN RESEARCH
- **Stage 2 generation:** BLOCKED
- **Generator implementation:** DEFERRED UNTIL STAGE 1 RESEARCH COMPLETION
- **Purpose:** define soluble-rock landscapes as coupled surface-water, groundwater, chemistry, fracture, void-growth, sediment, and collapse systems rather than random sinkholes or cave decorations.

## 1. Central rule

Karst is a hydrologic and geochemical network inside soluble material.

```text
soluble bedrock
+ fractures, bedding, porosity, and hydraulic gradient
+ chemically aggressive fluid
+ recharge and groundwater flow
→ dissolution and conduit enlargement
→ underground capture and drainage
→ springs, caves, sinkholes, collapse, and surface lowering
→ sediment trapping, rerouting, and later exposure
```

Surface morphology and subsurface drainage must be generated together.

## 2. Soluble materials

Earth karst commonly develops in:

- limestone;
- dolostone;
- gypsum;
- halite;
- locally other soluble or reactive rocks.

Potential hypothetical or planetary analogues may involve:

- water ice;
- carbon dioxide ice;
- sulfur or evaporitic minerals;
- hydrocarbon-soluble or exotic crustal materials;
- lava tubes, which resemble caves visually but are not dissolution karst.

WorldWright must preserve material/process identity rather than grouping all voids as caves.

## 3. Dissolution controls

Dissolution depends on:

- mineral solubility;
- fluid chemistry and acidity;
- temperature;
- carbon dioxide or other reactive species;
- residence time;
- flow rate;
- fracture surface area;
- saturation state;
- mixing of waters;
- biological activity where present.

High rainfall alone does not guarantee karst if rock is not soluble or flow cannot penetrate.

## 4. Fracture and bedding guidance

Groundwater commonly follows:

- joints;
- faults;
- bedding planes;
- contacts between permeable and impermeable units;
- paleokarst surfaces;
- brecciated zones;
- reef or evaporite fabrics.

Conduit networks therefore inherit geological structure and should not be isotropic random tunnels.

## 5. Recharge

Recharge may be:

- diffuse through soil and fractures;
- concentrated through sinkholes;
- captured from sinking streams;
- injected along faults;
- seasonal through snowmelt;
- episodic during storms;
- transferred from losing lakes or wetlands.

The proportion of diffuse versus focused recharge influences conduit growth and surface vulnerability.

## 6. Groundwater flow regimes

Karst flow may combine:

- slow diffuse matrix/fracture flow;
- rapid conduit flow;
- perched aquifers;
- phreatic saturated passages;
- vadose unsaturated shafts/canyons;
- epiphreatic passages near fluctuating water table;
- overflow routes activated during floods.

A single groundwater-height field cannot capture all conduit behavior, but a reduced network plus storage state can.

## 7. Conduit initiation and enlargement

Conduits can enlarge through positive feedback:

- slightly faster flow dissolves more rock;
- larger openings transmit more water;
- focused flow accelerates growth;
- breakthrough connects recharge to springs.

Potential outcomes:

- integrated cave networks;
- isolated cavities;
- master conduits with tributaries;
- abandoned higher-level passages after base-level fall;
- maze caves under diffuse or mixing conditions.

## 8. Surface karst forms

Potential landforms include:

- solutional grooves/karren;
- dolines/sinkholes;
- uvalas and poljes;
- disappearing streams/swallow holes;
- blind valleys;
- dry valleys;
- karst windows;
- springs;
- tower and cockpit karst;
- residual hills;
- turloughs/seasonal karst lakes;
- collapse depressions.

These forms emerge from structure, hydrology, climate, sediment, and landscape history.

## 9. Sinkhole types and evolution

Potential sinkhole processes include:

- solutional lowering;
- cover subsidence;
- cover collapse;
- bedrock collapse;
- suffosion/piping into voids;
- evaporite dissolution;
- groundwater-level change;
- cave-roof failure.

### Required distinction

A shallow solution depression differs from a catastrophic roof collapse.

Generator state should include:

- void/cavity support;
- cover thickness and cohesion;
- water-table history;
- sediment piping;
- collapse event and deposited/fallen material;
- open, filled, lake-occupied, or breached state.

## 10. Cave morphology

Cave systems may include:

- vadose canyons;
- phreatic tubes;
- shafts;
- collapse chambers;
- bedding-plane passages;
- fault-guided corridors;
- multi-level passages;
- speleothem-decorated inactive zones;
- underground rivers and lakes;
- sediment-filled or re-excavated passages.

A cave network needs hydraulic and geological logic, not decorative branching.

## 11. Base level and cave levels

Regional base level and water-table history strongly influence cave elevation and abandonment.

Potential sequence:

1. conduit develops near an old water table;
2. river incision or uplift lowers base level;
3. former conduit becomes vadose or abandoned;
4. new lower conduit develops;
5. older passages preserve sediment or speleothems;
6. collapse or erosion exposes cave remnants.

Multi-level cave systems therefore record landscape evolution.

## 12. Springs and resurgence

Karst aquifers often drain to focused springs.

Spring location depends on:

- hydraulic gradient;
- impermeable boundaries;
- valley incision;
- faults and contacts;
- conduit network;
- water-table state;
- sea/lake level.

Springs can migrate, dry, reverse, or become submarine with changing base level.

## 13. Surface–subsurface drainage exchange

Karst can produce:

- losing streams;
- sinking rivers;
- underground piracy;
- resurgent rivers;
- dry valleys;
- surface basins with hidden outlets;
- rapid flood transmission;
- groundwater-fed wetlands.

### Generator obligation

Drainage topology must support vertical links between surface channels and subsurface conduits.

A surface-only river solver cannot represent karst.

## 14. Sediment in karst systems

Sediment may:

- plug sinkholes and conduits;
- be flushed during floods;
- accumulate in caves;
- armor surfaces;
- cause suffosion and cover collapse;
- preserve paleoclimate and landscape records;
- emerge at springs;
- fill poljes and depressions.

Karst void volume and sediment storage must be reconciled.

## 15. Karst denudation and relief

Dissolution removes mass through solute export and can lower landscapes without large clastic sediment production.

Potential forms:

- broad low-relief solution plains;
- deeply dissected cone/tower karst;
- enclosed depressions;
- steep residual hills;
- subterranean drainage with few surface channels.

Climate and rock structure can produce very different terrain from the same soluble lithology.

## 16. Coastal and submarine karst

Changing sea level can:

- flood cave systems;
- create blue holes;
- shift freshwater–saltwater mixing zones;
- drive coastal dissolution;
- preserve drowned karst;
- cause collapse or sediment infill;
- create submarine springs.

Mixing corrosion and density stratification may create forms distinct from inland karst.

## 17. Evaporite karst

Gypsum and salt dissolve faster than carbonate rocks and may produce:

- rapid subsidence;
- collapse;
- sinkhole chains;
- disappearing streams;
- breccia pipes;
- highly unstable terrain;
- strong dependence on groundwater and climate.

A single limestone-based timescale is invalid for all soluble materials.

## 18. Pseudokarst and lookalikes

Similar morphology can arise from:

- lava tubes;
- thermokarst;
- mine collapse;
- impact pits;
- volcanic pit craters;
- piping in unconsolidated sediment;
- glacier moulins;
- tectonic fissures.

Stage 2 must include these negative analogues so shape alone cannot establish karst identity.

## 19. Planetary-regime branches

### Humid carbonate world

- strong chemical weathering;
- integrated caves and springs;
- tower/cone karst possible under suitable uplift and climate;
- rapid surface–subsurface exchange.

### Arid carbonate/evaporite world

- episodic recharge;
- long-lived cavities;
- salt/gypsum collapse;
- playa and groundwater interaction;
- flash-flood conduit flushing.

### Cold/periglacial branch

- seasonal recharge;
- freeze blockage;
- glacially inherited caves;
- thermokarst lookalikes;
- permafrost controlling groundwater pathways.

### Low-gravity branch

- altered roof stability and collapse geometry;
- fluid pressure/flow depends on gravity and atmosphere;
- large unsupported voids may persist where strength allows.

### High-gravity branch

- stronger overburden stress and collapse tendency;
- hydraulic gradients and conduit capacity differ;
- material strength and burial depth are essential.

### Ice-rich or cryokarst branch

Dissolution/sublimation of volatile solids may create pits, caves, and collapse, but the chemistry, thermal state, rheology, and flow medium must be explicitly distinct from carbonate karst.

### Airless branch

Liquid-water carbonate karst is forbidden without a stable reactive fluid. Sublimation, volatile loss, impact, or lava-tube processes require separate labels.

## 21. Generator obligation specification

### Canonical state required

- soluble material type and distribution;
- fracture/bedding/porosity architecture;
- fluid chemistry and saturation;
- recharge field and focused inputs;
- groundwater storage and hydraulic gradient;
- conduit/void graph;
- cave level and base-level history;
- surface–subsurface drainage links;
- sinkhole/collapse state;
- sediment plugging/storage;
- spring/resurgence outlets;
- dissolved mass export;
- active, fossil, drowned, filled, or reactivated state.

### Required process fields

- dissolution and saturation;
- fracture/conduit enlargement;
- recharge and groundwater routing;
- water-table/base-level migration;
- sediment piping and plugging;
- collapse and void support;
- spring discharge;
- surface capture and resurgence;
- coastal mixing or evaporite dissolution where enabled.

### Conservation requirement

```text
soluble rock removed
≈ dissolved export / precipitation elsewhere
+ suspended or collapsed sediment
+ enlarged void volume adjusted for collapse/fill
```

### Forbidden shortcuts

- soluble-rock label → random sinkholes;
- caves as generic noise tunnels;
- sinkhole collapse without subsurface void or material consequence;
- all depressions filled as lakes;
- surface drainage ignoring underground capture;
- thermokarst/lava tubes/impact pits classified as karst by shape.

## 22. Procedural failure signatures

- evenly spaced circular sinkholes;
- caves disconnected from recharge and springs;
- river disappears without a conduit destination;
- groundwater crosses impermeable boundaries freely;
- collapse produces no debris or void change;
- karst towers painted directly from lithology;
- dissolved mass vanishes from budget;
- identical behavior for limestone, gypsum, salt, and ice;
- present water table used for all fossil cave levels.

## 25. Conclusions safe enough to carry forward

### High confidence

- karst requires soluble material, reactive fluid, hydraulic gradients, and structural pathways;
- surface and underground drainage must be coupled;
- cave levels preserve base-level history;
- sinkhole processes include slow dissolution/subsidence and rapid collapse branches;
- dissolved mass and sediment/void state require conservation;
- morphology alone cannot distinguish karst from pseudokarst.

### Model-dependent or incomplete

- global conduit-network simplification;
- exotic-solvent karst;
- collapse thresholds;
- cross-planet chemistry;
- mixing-zone morphology.

### Not approved

- random sinkhole fields;
- generic cave noise;
- shape-only karst classification;
- generator implementation;
- image generation.
